# Episode 003 — Who Gets Blamed When Both Are Wrong

Approximately seventeen minutes (rendered runtime 17:18). A playful interviewer-style synthesis with Dr. Rowan Field, a fictional composite research correspondent. Rowan is not an author, no cited author was interviewed, and the dialogue is paraphrase rather than author testimony.

**AI-summary warning:** This episode is an AI-generated summary. It may contain errors and is an orientation, not a substitute for reading the source.

Episode 001 asked whether crowdsourced moral preferences should govern autonomous vehicles. Episode 002 followed the engineering that turns those preferences into a running decision procedure, and ended on the observation that oversight of such systems collapses to measurement after the fact. This episode asks where the obligation to measure would come from, and finds the candidate mechanism — public blame, feeding the tort system — has a systematic blind spot in exactly the dual-error cases real crashes tend to be.

## Focal source

1. Edmond Awad, Sydney Levine, Max Kleiman-Weiner, Sohan Dsouza, Joshua B. Tenenbaum, Azim Shariff, Jean-François Bonnefon, and Iyad Rahwan. "Drivers are blamed more than their automated cars when both make mistakes." *Nature Human Behaviour* 4, 2020, pp. 134–143 (published online 28 October 2019). [Zotero file](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/items/EIUADNKU) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-019-0762-8)

   **Version read:** the published article was not retrievable (paywalled; no open-access copy in Unpaywall or OpenAlex, and no PDF attachment in the Zotero library). All figures in this episode were read from the authors' **arXiv preprint**, `arXiv:1803.07170v2`, titled "Blaming humans in autonomous vehicle accidents: Shared responsibility across levels of automation" — same author list, same three studies, 40 pages. This is the **submitted version, not the version of record**; any change introduced during peer review is not reflected here. This is stated on air.

   - Supports: three vignette studies on US participants recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk, each scenario featuring a primary driver (tasked with driving) and a secondary driver (tasked with monitoring and intervening), with a pedestrian fatality; blame and causal responsibility each rated on 0–100 scales. **Every score quoted on air is the paper's combined blame-and-causal-responsibility measure**, and in study 2 the machine-side figure aggregates ratings of the car and of the manufacturer; this is stated once on air and carried through. Driver-type conditions map to automation levels: human–human (conventional), human primary/machine secondary (Guardian Angel), machine primary/human secondary (Autopilot), machine–machine (fully autonomous). Error conditions: **Bad Intervention** (primary acts correctly, secondary intervenes and kills — one agent errs) and **Missed Intervention** (primary heads toward a pedestrian, secondary fails to intervene — both agents err).
   - Sample sizes: study 1 (September 2017) 786 participants; study 2 (May 2017) 779 recruited, with machine side framed as either the car or the manufacturer; study 3, 973 recruited, adding the programmer as an industry representative and converting scenarios to genuine 1-vs-5 dilemmas. Regression models for studies 2 and 3 are **restricted by design to shared-control conditions** (382 and 586 participants respectively) — subsets, not attrition. Exclusion criteria: completion of all measures, near-perfect transcription of a 169-character CAPTCHA-style paragraph, and unique worker ID within study (all duplicates dropped).
   - Single-error case: the erring agent is blamed heavily and driver type does not matter — the paper’s "Mistake" coefficient is large and significant across all three studies (≈63 points in study 1, ≈56 in study 2, and a much smaller but still significant ≈11.6 in study 3, where the dilemma framing compresses the scale), while the human-versus-machine coefficient is small and **not** significant in any study. In shared control with only the machine at fault, human ≈53 and machine ≈50, not a significant gap.
   - Dual-error case: driver type becomes significant — being human adds ≈17 blame points in study 1 and ≈13 in study 2 for identical behaviour and outcome. The direction is **machines blamed less in the presence of a human co-driver**, established against two baselines: against the machine–machine car, a machine primary falls from ≈68 to ≈57 when its partner is human; against the fully autonomous car (≈75.6), the same software sharing control with a human draws ≈59.5 as primary and ≈48.5 as secondary.
   - Mechanism: the authors state explicitly that **why** machine drivers are blamed less in dual-error cases "remains an open question," and their own candidate explanation concerns uncertainty about machines' agential status rather than blame being transferred to the human. Any "absorption" or conserved-blame reading is an untested model and is not asserted on air. Perceived competence of humans and machines was comparable, measured both before exposure to the crash (study 1) and after (study 2), and did not mediate the blame effect. Residual blame attached to uninvolved parties — the car and manufacturer in the human-only condition, and the human passenger in the fully autonomous condition, which the authors interpret as entering the vehicle being read as accepting some responsibility.
   - Guardian Angel exception: in the human-primary/machine-secondary car under Bad Intervention, the machine draws **more** blame than the human — the only such case among the shared-control cars, i.e. where a human is actually a driver (supplemental tables show industry blamed above the user in the fully autonomous car too, where the human is a passenger) — which the authors attribute to the architecture assigning no agent to correct the monitor's own errors.
   - Policy argument: a risk of public **under-reaction** rather than over-reaction. Because US product safety is shaped substantially bottom-up through tort litigation, blame patterns that absolve the machine in dual-error cases weaken the liability pressure on manufacturers precisely where the software failed; no liability, legal-exposure or insurance variable was measured in any study — the inference from blame ratings to jury behaviour is the authors' forecast ("we expect that juries will be biased to absolve the car manufacturer"), and the episode marks it as such; the authors conclude that top-down regulation may be required and draw the parallel to automotive crashworthiness, which had to be imposed by statute in the 1960s rather than emerging from jury verdicts.
   - Boundary: **study 3 does not replicate the main effect** — in true 1-vs-5 dilemmas the dual-error driver-type effect is not significant, which the authors attribute to the dissolution of a clear "mistake" (and note Germany's ethics commission prohibits such life-for-life trade-offs outright). The finding should be read as holding for unambiguous-error cases and **not shown** to extend to dilemmas — a failure to demonstrate the effect there (Human = 2.341, SE 0.959), not a demonstration that it is absent.
   - Boundary (**this show's, not the authors'**): stylized vignettes with rating scales. A blame rating is not a jury verdict. The authors argue the contrary — "while we employed stylized, simplified vignettes in our research, our findings show external validity" — resting that claim on convergence with the reception of a single real crash; this episode declines to accept one news cycle as sufficient and says so on air.
   - Boundary (**this show's, not the authors'**): US residents only, recruited via Mechanical Turk. The authors do not raise this as a limitation — "USA residents" appears only as a neutral methods descriptor, and their sole generalisability caveat concerns other application domains rather than other countries. Given episodes 001 and 002 were built on documented cross-national variation in moral judgement about AVs, this episode flags it independently. Population language in the script is scoped to "these participants" / "US survey respondents" for measured results; "the public" appears only where the authors' own argument is being reported.
   - Boundary: the suggestion that blame patterns could become something drivers or purchasers strategically optimise against, and the comparison to judges deferring to actuarial pretrial risk assessments, are raised by the authors as speculation and analogy — not measured in these studies. The episode labels them as such.
