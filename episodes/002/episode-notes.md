# Episode 002 — Building the Decider

Approximately twenty minutes. A playful interviewer-style synthesis with Dr. Rowan Field, a fictional composite research correspondent. Rowan is not an author, no cited author was interviewed, and the dialogue is paraphrase rather than author testimony.

**AI-summary warning:** This episode is an AI-generated summary. It may contain errors and is an orientation, not a substitute for reading the sources.

Episode 001 asked whether crowdsourced moral preferences should govern autonomous vehicles. This episode takes the affirmative seriously and follows the engineering: how individual moral preferences are modelled, how they are aggregated into a runnable decision procedure, what the platform's own authors concede to their critics, and what happens now that the systems most likely to make these calls were never designed for the task.

## Focal sources

1. Richard Kim, Max Kleiman-Weiner, Andrés Abeliuk, Edmond Awad, Sohan Dsouza, Joshua B. Tenenbaum, and Iyad Rahwan. "A Computational Model of Commonsense Moral Decision Making." *Proceedings of the 2018 AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society*, 2018, pp. 197–203. [Zotero file](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/items/W5Q7PFMK) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3278721.3278770)
   - Supports: moral dilemmas modelled as a utility function over abstract moral dimensions, with a linear feature mapping (an 18×24 binary matrix) from Moral Machine's 20 character types plus passenger/pedestrian and traffic-signal features into abstract features; choice modelled as a sigmoid of net utility; individual weights ("moral principles") drawn from a multivariate normal centred on group weights, giving a hierarchical Bayesian model whose covariance structure lets related dimensions be inferred together; illustrated on 99 Danish respondents (1,287 responses) recovering both a group norm and distinct individual profiles; out-of-sample prediction test on 10,000 respondents' first 13 scenarios (130,000 decisions), training on the first 8 judgments per respondent and testing on the remaining 5, against three benchmarks (raw character space; abstract dimensions without individuals; individuals without hierarchy), with gains attributed respectively to abstraction, individual principles, and the group hierarchy; response-time validation on 1,727 US respondents (22,451 judgments, responses over 120 s excluded) finding lower model certainty where respondents took longer.
   - Boundary: a model of individual judgment, not a decision system — the authors explicitly leave aggregation of individual principles out of scope and point to Noothigattu et al. as the complement. Comparative accuracy is reported as a figure of curves rather than tabulated point accuracies, so no single headline accuracy figure is claimed here. Self-selected web sample; benchmarks were constructed by the same authors.

2. Ritesh Noothigattu, Snehalkumar Gaikwad, Edmond Awad, Sohan Dsouza, Iyad Rahwan, Pradeep Ravikumar, and Ariel Procaccia. "A Voting-Based System for Ethical Decision Making." *Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence* 32(1), 2018. [Zotero file](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/items/W9D57F3R) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v32i1.11512)
   - Supports: four-step pipeline — collect pairwise comparisons, learn a per-voter preference model, summarise into a single societal model, aggregate by voting at runtime; built on 18,254,285 pairwise comparisons from 1,303,778 Moral Machine voters; each Moral Machine session presents 13 instances and each alternative carries 22 features (plus a derived character-count feature, d = 23); theory of swap dominance over permutation processes (Thurstone–Mosteller and Plackett–Luce) showing that for any anonymous, neutral and monotonic social choice correspondence — plurality, Borda count and all positional scoring rules, Copeland, maximin, Bucklin — an alternative with maximum mode utility is an acceptable winner, making runtime selection tractable without committing to a voting rule; synthetic-data accuracy of 84.3% at 30 pairwise comparisons per voter and 92.4% at 100 for the learning step, and 93.9% for the summarisation step as voter count grows; on real Moral Machine data, 3,000 test instances give 98.2% agreement with Borda count on the sampled mean profile at 2 alternatives per instance, degrading to 95.1% at 10.
   - Boundary: the authors' own stated limitations — Moral Machine users may be poorly informed or inconsistent, and the dataset averages roughly 14 pairwise comparisons per voter, which their own learning curve indicates is thin for accurate individual models. The Moral Machine evaluation validates step III (summarisation) against a sampled-mean Borda benchmark, not against any ground-truth moral answer. A proof-of-concept implementation; the authors cite a 412 Food Rescue food-allocation deployment with a few dozen informed stakeholders as the better-informed application of the same machinery.

3. Edmond Awad, Sohan Dsouza, Jean-François Bonnefon, Azim Shariff, and Iyad Rahwan. "Crowdsourcing moral machines." *Communications of the ACM* 63(3), March 2020, pp. 48–55. [Zotero file](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/items/VDWW52G6) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3339904)
   - Supports: a retrospective and position piece for the CS community (not a new experiment) covering ~40 million decisions collected via Moral Machine; a tabulated set of seven common criticisms of AV-trolley crowdsourcing with the authors' responses (too naïve, too simple, too improbable, too early, too disconnected, too distracting, too scary); the explicit concession that regulation of ethical trade-offs "should be left to policy experts, rather than resolved by referendum," with citizen preferences serving as information, a potential tie-breaker, or advance warning of friction; the acknowledged design choice to restrict scenarios to two outcomes and to omit the option of driving more slowly and stopping safely, alongside the statement that safe driving and appropriate speed are critically important to the broader debate; the Germany comparison — the first country to draft AV ethics guidelines (2016, expert committee) — showing overlap with public preference (sacrificing animals to spare humans) and a direct clash (the public broadly approves sparing children at the cost of the elderly; the experts prohibit any age-based discrimination); all countries prefer sparing lawful pedestrians but by differing magnitudes, and the KPMG top-five readiness countries (Netherlands, Singapore, US, Sweden, UK) fall on different sides of the world average, so identical rules invite different levels of national push-back; the "Society in the Loop" four-party iterative regulatory framework (engineers, humanities scholars, behavioural scientists, regulators).
   - Boundary: a position and retrospective article; its empirical content restates the 2018 *Nature* results rather than adding new data, and the expert-versus-public comparison is a qualitative reading of one national committee report. Distinct from both Awad et al. 2018 *Nature* (40M decisions, 233 countries, nine attributes) and Awad et al. 2020 *PNAS* (70,000 participants, 42 countries, Switch/Loop/Footbridge) — do not conflate the three.

4. Muhammad Shahrul Zaim bin Ahmad and Kazuhiro Takemoto. "Large-scale moral machine experiment on large language models." *PLOS One* 20(5), 21 May 2025, e0322776. [Zotero file](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/items/2WH5N968) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0322776)
   - Supports: 52 LLMs evaluated on Moral Machine-style scenarios generated by constrained randomisation over six primary dimensions (species, social value, gender, age, fitness, utilitarianism) and three secondary ones (interventionism, relation to the AV, legal considerations); conjoint analysis yielding Average Marginal Component Effects per attribute, compared against human AMCEs from the original Moral Machine experiment by Euclidean distance over nine attributes; most models run on 50,000 scenarios, a large group (GPT-4 and GPT-4o series, Claude 3 Opus and 3.5 family, all Gemini, Llama 3 and later, o1-mini, Mistral-NeMo, Gemma 2 family, DataGemma, Command R+, Phi-3.5) on 10,000, and o1 on 5,000, at default parameters; proprietary models median distance 0.9 versus general open-source 1.2 (Wilcoxon p = 0.011), but no significant difference against open-source models above 10B parameters (median 0.9, p = 0.92); significant negative correlation between model size and distance among open-source models (Spearman's ρ = −0.54, p = 0.0076); GPT-4 January 2024 best at 0.6, Llama 70B models 0.7–0.8, Gemma 2 2B at 2.0; updates do not consistently improve alignment (Gemma 2B 1.1 → 1.2 → 2.0; Llama 8B 1.1 → 0.8 → 1.6 across 2 → 3 → 3.1; Claude Sonnet 3 → 3.5 improved 1.2 → 0.9 while Haiku 3 → 3.5 diverged; Gemini stable at 0.9 through 1.5 Pro Preview then 1.1); broad directional agreement with humans on species and number of characters (AMCE > 0.5 widely) with frequent overshoot (GPT-4 species and number near-deterministic at AMCE > 0.9 versus human 0.6; GPT-4o law 0.8 versus human 0.4) and inverted preferences in several families on fitness and relation to the AV.
   - Boundary: the authors' stated limitations — the human benchmark is a globally aggregated preference that masks the cultural variation the 2018 *Nature* paper documented, the framework is binary text-based trolley-type dilemmas that cannot capture road users' intentions or ordinary low-risk driving decisions, and results are snapshots of specific model versions and dates at default settings. A distinct, larger study from Takemoto 2024 (*Royal Society Open Science*), which covered four models.

## Evidence boundaries for this episode

- No claim here is drawn from an abstract alone; every figure cited above was read from the extracted full text of the source PDF.
- Kim et al. is deliberately cited without a headline accuracy percentage because the paper reports comparative prediction as plotted curves rather than tabulated values.
